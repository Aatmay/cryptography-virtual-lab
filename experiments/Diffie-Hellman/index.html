🎯 Experiment Aim

To demonstrate and verify the Diffie–Hellman Key Exchange algorithm by allowing two parties (Alice and Bob) to securely establish a shared symmetric cryptographic key over an unencrypted, public communication channel without transmitting the key itself.

📋 Learning Objectives

  • Understand the role of asymmetric one-way mathematical functions in secure symmetric key agreement.
  • Apply modular exponentiation using public domain parameters: prime modulus (P) and generator primitive root (G).
  • Simulate the computation of private-to-public key pairs: x = G^a mod P and y = G^b mod P.
  • Trace transmission across an eavesdropped public network and verify why an observer cannot compute the private keys or shared secret due to the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP).
  • Mathematically prove and confirm key agreement equivalence: k_a = k_b = G^(a·b) mod P.

Theoretical Background: Diffie–Hellman Protocol

Diffie–Hellman key exchange is an asymmetric cryptographic mechanism developed by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976. It provides a means for two communicative entities to establish a shared symmetric cryptographic key over an unencrypted communication channel.

2. Mathematical Proof of Equivalence

Both parties arrive at the identical secret key because of modular exponentiation power laws:

Alice computes: ka = (y)a mod P = (Gb)a mod P = Ga · b mod P
Bob computes: kb = (x)b mod P = (Ga)b mod P = Ga · b mod P
Since both expressions evaluate to Ga · b mod P, it follows that:
ka = kb

Laboratory Procedure

  1. Parameter Agreement: Both endpoints agree upon global public parameters: prime modulus P and primitive root G.
  2. Private Key Selection: Alice chooses private integer a (where a < P). Bob chooses private integer b (where b < P).
  3. Public Key Generation: Alice computes x = Ga mod P. Bob computes y = Gb mod P.
  4. Key Exchange: Alice and Bob send their generated keys x and y over the insecure channel. Alice receives y, and Bob receives x.
  5. Secret Key Derivation: Alice calculates ka = ya mod P. Bob calculates kb = xb mod P. Verify that ka = kb.

Test Your Understanding

Answer at least 4 out of 5 questions correctly (80% passing grade) to complete the assessment and unlock the references.

1. On which specific mathematical hardness assumption does the fundamental security of the Diffie–Hellman key exchange rely?




2. Why must the generator G strictly be a primitive root modulo the prime P?




3. If Eve captures P, G, x = Ga mod P, and y = Gb mod P, why can't she simply compute (x · y) mod P to derive the shared secret key?




4. If a prime modulus P is selected such that (P - 1) factors entirely into very small prime factors (smooth integer), which cryptographic attack makes solving the private keys trivial?
